OverviewOverview

One idea runs through the whole chapter

Scarcity→Choice→Opportunity cost→What · How · For whom?→Economic system→Goods the market gets wrong

Resources are scarce, so every agent must choose. Every choice has an opportunity cost. Society must decide what, how and for whom to produce, through a market, planned or mixed system. The market fails for public, merit and demerit goods. The PPC shows the first four links at once.

1.1 · Syllabus 1.1.1–1.1.3Concept

Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

Definition · Scarcity 1.1.1

A situation where there are insufficient resources to satisfy all the needs and wants of people. Resources are finite; wants are unlimited.

  • Applies to every agent: individuals, firms, governments, international agencies.
  • Needs are essentials (food, shelter, clothing). Wants are less important desires (a new TV).
  • Scarcity is relative, not a shortage. Even rich countries face it because wants keep growing.
Definition · Opportunity cost 1.1.3

The benefit forgone from the next best alternative when a choice is made. One alternative, not the sum of all of them.

AgentChoiceOpportunity cost
StudentMovie, homework or friends; picks movie, homework 2ndThe homework
Government$10m: hospital (1st) or university (2nd)The university
FirmNew IT system or new factory; picks ITThe factory
Exam trap"The opportunity cost of the movie is the homework and friends" is wrong: only the next best counts. And opportunity cost is not the money price: a free concert ticket still costs your time.
1.1 · Syllabus 1.1.4Concept

The three basic questions of resource allocation

What to produce?

Which goods and how many. Consumer goods (give utility directly: phones, curry) vs capital goods (make other goods: machines, factories). Should Bata Malaysia make sports shoes, casual shoes or sandals?

How to produce?

Which mix of factors. Labour-intensive: high labour relative to capital (hand-stitched shoes). Capital-intensive: high capital relative to labour (machine-made). Firms pick the cheapest efficient method.

For whom to produce?

Who gets the output, which decides how equal society is. Market: whoever has purchasing power. Planned: whoever the state decides.

Links to 1.4The answers depend on the economic system: the price mechanism (market), a central planning authority (command), or both (mixed).
1.2 · Syllabus 1.2.1Concept

How economists think

  • Social: it studies human behaviour and how society works.
  • Science: observe behaviour, form a theory, test it.
  • A theory is usually expressed as a model: a simplified representation of reality. The PPC and demand–supply diagram are both models.
Evaluation hookEconomists can't run controlled experiments on whole economies and people don't always behave predictably, so predictions are less certain than in natural sciences. That's why assumptions like ceteris paribus are needed.

Micro · mikros, small

Individual markets and decision-makers: households, firms, one labour market. Demand for durians; demand for nurses.

Macro · makros, large

The whole economy via aggregates: AD, national output, inflation, unemployment, growth, trade.

The line is blurred: a rise in demand for cars (micro) can change a country's imports and exports (macro).

1.2 · Syllabus 1.2.2Concept

Positive vs normative statements

Positive · fact-based

Objective; can be tested, verified or refuted by evidence. May be true or false. Often cause-and-effect. "Malaysia's population in 2019 was over 30 million."

Normative · value judgement

Subjective opinion about good/bad, fair/unfair. Cannot be tested. Often contains should, ought, fair, better. "The government should spend more on education."

Exam traps (1) A positive statement can be false and still be positive. (2) A forecast is positive. (3) "If firms raise prices, profits will rise" is positive. (4) Questions too: "What is the impact of removing petrol subsidies?" is positive; "How should the government remove subsidies?" is normative.
In essaysWhen a question asks whether something is "desirable" or "should" happen, say the answer partly depends on a value judgement about equity vs efficiency. That is AO3.
1.2 · Syllabus 1.2.3–1.2.4Concept

Ceteris paribus and time periods

Definition · Ceteris paribus Latin

"All other things remaining equal." The effect of one variable is considered in isolation while every other influence is held constant.

It isolates one cause, e.g. a price change's effect on quantity demanded with income, tastes and other prices unchanged. In reality many things change at once: a standard limitation of any model.

PeriodDefinitionHow output rises
Short runAt least one factor fixed (usually capital or land)Only by adding the variable factor, typically labour
Long runAll factors variable; technology constantChange quantity and quality of all inputs
Very long runTechnology, regulation and social attitudes also changeTechnical progress; policy change; social change (WW1 brought more women into work)
Most reusable evaluationShort run vs long run. In Chapter 1: a PPC shift needs the long run; a transition economy suffers in the short run but may gain later; specialisation raises output now but can de-skill workers.

1.3 · Syllabus 1.3.2–1.3.5Concept

The entrepreneur, and two kinds of capital

1 · Organises

Decides how to combine land, labour and capital to raise efficiency and output: the "how to produce" question at firm level.

2 · Bears risk

Invests own funds under uncertainty. Risk-taking separates the entrepreneur from other workers; profit is the reward.

Enterprise drives innovation, jobs and growth, so an "enterprise culture" (start-up grants, easier regulation) shifts the PPC out. In Malaysia: SME and start-up support via MDEC and Cradle.

Physical capital 1.3.2

Man-made factors: machinery, buildings, infrastructure. Often the main source of growth in low- and lower-middle-income countries.

Human capital

Skills, knowledge and experience of the labour force, built through education and training.

Growth, two waysBoth raise labour productivity, so both shift the PPC outward. Human capital links to merit goods (education) in 1.6.
1.3 · Syllabus 1.3.4Concept

Specialisation and the division of labour

Definition · Specialisation

Individuals, firms, regions and economies concentrate on producing the goods and services where they have an advantage over others.

By firm (tyre makers), by region (semiconductors around Penang), by country (Brazil and coffee), by individual = division of labour.

Definition · Division of labour

Production is split into many small tasks and each worker specialises in one task.

Adam Smith's pin factory (1776): one worker doing every step makes about 1 pin a day; split into 18 operations, output rose to about 5,000 pins per worker per day.

Advantages

  • Workers do what they're best at → higher productivity
  • Training in one task only → lower training costs
  • No time lost switching tasks
  • Production lines and machinery → lower average costs

Disadvantages

  • Monotony can reduce productivity
  • Narrow skills if made redundant
  • One group's strike can halt the line
  • Identical, standardised products

1.4 · Syllabus 1.4.1–1.4.2Concept

Market vs planned economy

Market economy

Advantages

  • Efficiency: profit motive cuts waste, drives R&D
  • Consumer sovereignty
  • No allocating bureaucracy
  • More choice, increased by trade
  • Economic and political freedom

Disadvantages (each a market failure)

  • Negative externalities (pollution, deforestation)
  • Monopoly power
  • Public goods not provided
  • Merit goods under-provided
  • Inequality: the rich have more "votes"
  • Instability: no full-employment guarantee

Planned economy

Advantages

  • More equal distribution; minimum living standard
  • No private monopoly exploitation
  • Stability: low unemployment
  • More public and merit goods

Disadvantages

  • No economic freedom
  • Inefficiency, little innovation, weak incentives
  • Bureaucracy and corruption
  • Planning errors without prices → shortages and surpluses
1.4 · Syllabus 1.4.2Concept

Mixed economy

Definition · Mixed economy

Resources are allocated partly by the price mechanism in the private sector and partly by government planning in the public sector.

All real economies are mixed; they differ in the degree of intervention.

What government does

  • Protects property rights and contracts; keeps markets competitive
  • Provides public goods (defence, street lights)
  • Corrects information failure: encourages merit goods, discourages demerit goods (taxes, subsidies, information, regulation, direct provision)
  • Deals with externalities; redistributes income; stabilises the economy

Advantages

Market efficiency plus correction of market failure; less inequality; variety of private, public and merit goods; more stability.

Disadvantages

Fear of nationalisation deters investment; higher taxes cut incentives; corruption and rent-seeking; government failure.

Malaysian applicationPrivate firms dominate manufacturing and services; the state controls strategic firms (Petronas, TNB, GLCs); public clinics and schools; price controls and subsidies. Targeted diesel (2024) and RON95 (2025) subsidies shift the balance back towards the price mechanism while protecting lower-income households.
1.4 · Syllabus 1.4.2Concept

Transition economies

Definition · Transition economy

An economy moving from central planning to a market-oriented system: Russia and Central/Eastern Europe after 1991, China from 1978, Vietnam (Đổi Mới) from 1986.

The process

  • Privatise; allow new firms and FDI
  • Liberalise trade (Poland, Russia, Estonia abolished tariffs in 1992)
  • Abolish planning; free prices
  • Build banks, a stock exchange
  • Enforce private property law

Difficulties

  • Sharp fall in GDP
  • Lack of entrepreneurial skill
  • High unemployment (inside the PPC)
  • High inflation as prices are freed
  • Unfair privatisation, inequality
  • Less welfare; trade deficits
EvaluationSpeed matters: "shock therapy" brings short-run unemployment and inflation but can raise long-run efficiency; China's gradual approach avoided Russia's deep fall in output.
Handout correctionEconomic System Part 2 says land and capital in a market economy are "predominantly owned by the public sector". It should read private sector. Check with Puan Zaihan.
1.5 · Syllabus 1.5.1Concept

Production possibility curves

Definition · PPC 1.5.1

A curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all resources are fully and efficiently employed, given the state of technology.

Four assumptions: full employment · fixed resources · constant technology · two goods only.

Diagram markLabel axes with the two named goods ("Capital goods", "Consumer goods"), never X and Y. Label the curve PPC, the origin O, and every point you discuss.
1.5 · Syllabus 1.5.1Concept

What the PPC illustrates

ConceptHow the PPC shows it
ScarcityPoints outside (H) can't be produced.
ChoiceThe economy must pick one point on the curve.
Opportunity costThe negative slope: more of one good means less of the other.
EfficiencyEvery point on the curve is productively efficient.
UnemploymentPoints inside (U): idle or misused resources.
GrowthShort run: U towards the curve. Long run: the curve shifts out.
OC of 1 more X = units of Y given up ÷ units of X gained
MCQ methodIn diagram MCQs the opportunity cost is a distance on the other axis. Drop guide lines from the start and end points to the axis of the good given up and read off the gap.
1.5 · Syllabus 1.5.2Concept

The shape follows opportunity cost

Concave · rising OC

The usual case. Resources are specialised, so moving them to the good they suit less loses ever more of the other good.

Straight · constant OC

Resources equally efficient at both goods. Handout Table 2: each extra X costs 2 Y.

Convex · falling OC

Resources get better at X as more is made (economies of scale). Rare in exams.

1.5 · Syllabus 1.5.3Concept

Shifts of the PPC

Outward: causes

  • Better technology, more capital-intensive methods
  • New resources (mineral deposits)
  • Bigger labour force: births, net immigration, later retirement
  • Better human capital
  • Enterprise culture

Inward: causes

  • Disasters or war destroying capital
  • Depletion of natural resources
  • Net emigration; shorter legal working week
  • Cuts to education and training
Shift vs movementHigher unemployment does not shift the PPC; it moves the economy inside it. Reallocating resources is a movement along it. Only a change in the quantity or quality of resources, or technology, shifts it.

If only one good's resources or technology improve, only that intercept moves. If working hours are cut by law, the PPC shifts in more for the more labour-intensive good.

1.5 · Syllabus 1.5.4Concept

A point inside the PPC

Inside the curve, resources are unemployed or used inefficiently. Output of both goods could rise at zero opportunity cost. Reducing unemployment is "free" growth in the short run; moving along the curve always costs something.

Capital vs consumer goods: today vs the future

More capital goods now means less consumption today, but the PPC shifts out further later. Mostly consumer goods means more now and slower growth.

EvaluationThe "best" point depends on whether society values present or future consumption: a normative judgement.
1.6 · Syllabus 1.6.1Concept

Free goods vs economic goods

All goods→Free goodsEconomic goods→Private · Public (pure / quasi) · Merit · Demerit
Free good 1.6.1

Unlimited in supply, needs no factors of production, so zero opportunity cost and no price: air, sunlight, seawater.

Economic good

Limited because it uses scarce resources, so it has an opportunity cost. Includes private, public, merit and demerit goods.

Trap"Free of charge" isn't a free good. A toy in a cereal box used materials and labour. Free public healthcare is an economic good, paid for by taxes.
Definition · Private good

Excludable (non-payers can be stopped) and rival (one person's use reduces what's left). Also rejectable. A plate of nasi goreng in the canteen.

1.6 · Syllabus 1.6.2Concept

Public goods and the free rider problem

Definition · Public good 1.6.2

Non-excludable (non-payers can't be stopped from benefiting) and non-rival (the marginal cost of an extra user is zero). Usually non-rejectable. Defence, street lights, flood control, lighthouses.

RivalNon-rival
ExcludablePrivate: food, petrolClub / toll: pay-TV, uncongested toll road
Non-excludableCommon resource: crowded beach, fish in the seaPure public: defence, flood control
Non-excludable→people free ride→no effective demand revealed→firms can't profit→missing market

Non-rivalry adds to this: with zero marginal cost, the efficient price is zero, so no profit-seeking firm supplies it. Government provides public goods, financed by taxation.

Definition · Free rider

Someone who enjoys a good's benefit without paying, because they can't be excluded.

1.6 · Syllabus 1.6.2Concept

Quasi-public goods

Definition · Quasi-public good

Some but not all features of a public good. Semi-non-rival (can become congested: parks, beaches, roads at peak times) and/or semi-non-excludable (exclusion possible but costly: fencing a beach, toll booths).

Because exclusion is possible, the private sector can provide some quasi-public goods, such as the PLUS expressways.

Malaysian exampleKL's SMART Tunnel: as a flood-diversion tunnel it acts like a public good, but its motorway section charges a toll, so that part is excludable.
1.6 · Syllabus 1.6.3–1.6.4Concept

Merit and demerit goods

Definition · Merit good 1.6.3

A private good considered desirable but under-consumed and under-provided by the free market because of information failure: consumers underestimate its benefits. Often has positive externalities (SB > PB). Education, healthcare, vaccinations.

Education: private benefits (skills, pay) plus external benefits (skilled workforce, growth) that individuals ignore. Vaccination protects others too.

Definition · Demerit good 1.6.4

A private good considered undesirable that is over-consumed because consumers underestimate its harm. Often has negative externalities. Cigarettes, alcohol, junk food.

Malaysian policy: excise duty on sugar-sweetened drinks (since 2019), cigarette taxes, the Control of Smoking Products for Public Health Act 2024.

Don't confuseMerit goods are not public goods. Education is rival and excludable. Public goods: the market provides none. Merit goods: it provides too little.
Forward link1.6 is the entry point to Chapter 3 (market failure and government intervention).
Worked example 1 · 1.5Worked example

Calculate opportunity cost from a PPC table

PointGood XGood Y
A015
B114
C212
D39
E45
F50

Q: What is the opportunity cost of moving from C to D? What happens to opportunity cost along the curve, and why?

  1. Write the formula: OC of one more X = Y given up ÷ X gained.
  2. C → D: Y falls from 12 to 9, so 3 units of Y are given up; X rises from 2 to 3, so 1 unit gained.
  3. OC = 3 ÷ 1 = 3 units of Y per unit of X. State it as a positive amount of the good given up.
  4. Repeat for each step: A→B 1, B→C 2, C→D 3, D→E 4, E→F 5 units of Y.
  5. Each extra unit of X costs more Y: increasing opportunity cost, so the PPC is concave. Resources suited to Y are moved into X last.
Handout noteThe handout writes these as −1 … −5 (the slope). In an answer, write "the opportunity cost of the 3rd unit of X is 3 units of Y".
Worked example 2 · 1.5Worked example

Straight-line PPC: what is given up?

Q (PPC pack Q8): A PPC is a straight line from 1,000 units of Y (vertical intercept) to 2,000 units of X. The economy produces 1,200 X on the PPC. How much Y has it given up?

  1. Find the constant opportunity cost: 1,000 Y ÷ 2,000 X = 0.5 Y per unit of X.
  2. Y given up for 1,200 X: 1,200 × 0.5 = 600 Y.
  3. Check: it still produces 1,000 − 600 = 400 Y.
  4. Answer 600. The trap option is 400: that's what it still makes, not what it gave up.
Worked example 3 · 1.1Worked example

Apply opportunity cost to a government [4]

Q: Malaysia's government has RM2 billion for either targeted fuel subsidies or new rural clinics. Explain opportunity cost using this example and say why it applies even though the government can borrow.

  1. Define [1]: opportunity cost is the benefit of the next best alternative forgone.
  2. Apply [1]: if clinics are first priority, the opportunity cost is the benefit the fuel subsidy would have given (lower transport costs for households).
  3. One alternative [1]: it is that one next-best option, not every possible use of RM2 billion.
  4. Borrowing [1]: borrowed funds must be repaid from future taxes, so future spending is forgone; and the doctors, builders and land used for clinics can't be used elsewhere at the same time.
Worked example 4 · 1.6 · Paper 2Worked example

Is a beach a public good? [8]

Q: Explain the difference between a public good and a private good and consider the extent to which a beach could be described as a public good.

  1. AO1: define rival/non-rival and excludable/non-excludable.
  2. AO1: apply: national defence (non-rival, non-excludable) vs nasi goreng (rival, excludable). Free rider → market provides no public goods.
  3. AO2: a quiet public beach (Port Dickson on a weekday) is largely non-rival and hard to fence off: close to a public good.
  4. AO2: but when crowded it becomes rival, and a resort-owned beach in Langkawi with entry fees is excludable: like a private good.
  5. AO3: it doesn't fully meet both criteria. The extent depends on congestion and ownership. Judgement: a quasi-public good.
Paper 2 · Exam techniqueExam

Turning Chapter 1 into essay marks

AO1 · Knowledge

Precise definitions, correct terms, an accurate fully labelled diagram.

AO2 · Analysis

Developed chains linking cause to effect, using the diagram.

AO3 · Evaluation

Say what the outcome depends on and why, then a justified conclusion that answers the exact question.

PartMarking
(a) 8 marks
Explain… and consider…		Point-based: AO1 3 · AO2 3 · AO3 2. One AO3 mark is reserved for a justified conclusion.
(b) 12 marks
Assess…		Levels: AO1+AO2 /8 (L3 6–8, L2 3–5, L1 1–2) and AO3 /4. A one-sided answer gets 0 for AO3.

Skeletons

8-marker · ~20 min

  • Define every key term
  • Diagram with named axes, referred to
  • 2–3 linked points from the diagram
  • One specific "however" + a one-line conclusion

12-marker · ~30 min

  • Intro: define, say what it depends on
  • Side 1: 2–3 developed chains
  • Side 2: 2–3 chains against
  • Weigh: which matters more, when, for whom
  • Conclusion answering the exact words

Paper 2 is 1 h 45 min for 40 marks: about 2.5 minutes per mark.

Paper 2 · Exam techniqueExam

Build chains, then weigh them

Weak · L1 assertion

"Free markets cause pollution."

Strong · L3 chain

Firms maximise profit, so they ignore costs imposed on third parties such as river pollution from palm oil mills. Because external costs aren't in the price, output is above the social optimum: resources are over-allocated.

Paper 2 · MistakesMistakes

Common examiner-report errors

  • Axes labelled X and Y, or "good A / good B", when the question names the goods.
  • Saying unemployment shifts the PPC inward. It moves the economy inside the curve.
  • Calling opportunity cost the money price, or listing all alternatives instead of the next best.
  • Calling money "capital", or a merit good a public good.
  • Defining a public good as "provided by the government". Use non-excludable and non-rival.
  • Listing advantages and disadvantages without explaining any (stuck at L1–L2).
  • A conclusion that doesn't answer the question. If it asks "always", say "not always" and when.
  • One-sided 12-mark answers: AO3 = 0.
Command wordsExplain: definition + chain + diagram, not a list. Consider: one reasoned "however" + a one-line judgement. Assess: both sides weighed, then a conclusion that answers "always?", "only?", "whether?".
Practice · 1.2–1.5Practice

Practice questions · Paper 1 style

Q1 · 1.2[1]

Which is a normative statement? (A) If firms spend more on advertising, sales may not rise. (B) If firms raise prices, profits will rise. (C) The government should reduce taxes on spending so that economic welfare will rise. (D) Export volumes will rise if the government lowers the exchange rate.

C"Should" signals a value judgement. A, B and D are cause-and-effect claims that can be tested, even if false.
Q2 · 1.3[1]

Meera finished art college and starts a business making clay figures. Which factors of production must she buy from other businesses?

Land and capitalShe supplies her own labour (artistic skill) and enterprise (organising, taking the risk). She rents premises and buys clay (land) and a kiln and tools (capital).
Q3 · 1.4[1]

A country moving from a planned to a market economy sells its transport services to private firms. Most likely consequence? (A) Government ownership increases. (B) Public sector services increase. (C) Central planning increases. (D) Consumer sovereignty increases.

DPrivatisation moves allocation from planners to the price mechanism, so consumer spending decides what firms provide.
Q4 · 1.5[1]

A PPC for capital and consumer goods shifts inward. Which could have caused it? (A) A rise in the retirement age. (B) More investment. (C) More net emigration. (D) Technological progress.

CNet emigration cuts the quantity of labour, so productive capacity falls. A, B and D shift the PPC outward.
Practice · Paper 2Practice

Practice · Paper 2 essays

Plan each in 5 minutes, then compare.

Q5 · 1.5[8]

With the help of a PPC diagram, explain the difference between actual and potential economic growth and consider whether an economy should devote more resources to capital goods.

PlanDefine both; PPC with U → V (actual, zero OC) and PPC1 → PPC2 (potential). More capital goods: less consumption now, faster outward shift later. Consider: depends on time preference (normative) and whether the capital is productive; a poor economy may not afford lower consumption now.
Q6 · 1.3[8]

Explain how the division of labour can increase productivity and consider whether it always benefits a firm.

PlanDefine; pin factory; expertise, no switching time, machinery. Consider: needs a large market for high volume; monotony and strikes can cut productivity; narrow skills. Conclude: benefits a firm with high volume and repetitive tasks, less so for small or creative output.
Q7 · 1.4[12]

Assess whether a planned economy is more likely than a market economy to achieve a fair distribution of resources.

Plan"Fair" is normative: define it (equality of outcome vs reward for effort). For: state distributes evenly, rations essentials, provides merit goods. Against: planning errors cause shortages, so fairness on paper isn't delivered; corruption favours insiders; mixed economies redistribute via tax and welfare while keeping incentives. Judge: planned systems aim at equal outcomes but often fail to deliver them; a mixed economy is more likely to achieve a fair and efficient result.
Q8 · 1.6[12]

Assess whether governments should provide merit goods free of charge.

PlanFor: information failure and positive externalities mean under-consumption; free provision raises use and equity. Against: zero price → over-use and waiting lists; tax cost and its opportunity cost; government failure. Evaluate: depends on cause (information → campaigns may be enough; low income → subsidies or free provision). Judge with the cause in mind.
