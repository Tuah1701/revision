What the chapter is really about

A force can change an object's shape as well as its motion. Chapter 6 asks: how much does it stretch, does it come back, and how much energy does it store?

Force ⇒ deformation→Hooke's law \(F = kx\)→Stress, strain, \(E\)→Elastic or plastic?→Energy \(\tfrac12Fx\)

6.1 Stress and strainConcept

Two ways to deform in one dimension

Tensile force (tension)Compressive force
Forcespull outwards, away from each otherpush inwards, towards each other
Effectlonger and thinnershorter and fatter
Length\(L_0 + \Delta L\)\(L_0 - \Delta L\)
Examplesteel cable, bottom of a loaded beambuilding column, top of a loaded beam
Loaded beamA beam bent by a load: the top surface is compressed (particles closer), the bottom is stretched (particles further apart), and the neutral axis in the middle doesn't change length.
Why it springs backDisplaced molecules feel a restoring force pulling them back to their equilibrium separation. That is what makes a material elastic.
6.1 Stress and strainConcept

Definitions to memorise

Load

The force applied to an object that causes it to deform.

Extension

The increase in length of an object when a tensile force is applied: \(x = L - L_0\).

Compression

The decrease in length of an object when a compressive force is applied.

Limit of proportionality

The point beyond which the extension is no longer proportional to the load (Hooke's law no longer applies).

TrapExtension is the change in length, not the new length. Read both scale positions and subtract.
6.1 Stress and strainConcept

Hooke's law

Hooke's law

The extension of an object is directly proportional to the load applied, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.

\(F = kx\)
P ≠ EBetween the limit of proportionality and the elastic limit the graph is curved (no Hooke's law) but the object still returns to its original length.
6.1 Stress and strainConcept

The spring constant \(k\)

Spring constant

The force per unit extension: \(k = \dfrac{F}{x}\), unit N m⁻¹. It measures stiffness.

Bigger \(k\) ⇒ more force needed for the same extension ⇒ stiffer. It's the gradient of F–x (or 1 ÷ gradient of x–F).

FactorChangeEffect on \(k\)
Materialstronger bonding between atomslarger
Coil diameterlarger diametersmaller
Wire thicknessthicker wirelarger
Natural lengthlonger springsmaller
Fix in your notes\(k\) is not "how easy it is to break". That's strength. \(k\) is force per unit extension.

Combining springs

Series (end to end)Parallel (side by side)
Force in eachsame \(F\) in bothshared: \(F/2\) each (identical)
Extensionadd: \(x = x_1 + x_2\)same \(x\) for both
Rule\(\dfrac1{k_{\text{eff}}} = \dfrac1{k_1} + \dfrac1{k_2}\)\(k_{\text{eff}} = k_1 + k_2\)
Two identical\(k_{\text{eff}} = \dfrac k2\) (less stiff)\(k_{\text{eff}} = 2k\) (stiffer)
Careful wordingThe individual springs don't change. It's the arrangement that is stiffer or less stiff.
Memory hookSpring rules are the opposite of resistor rules: springs in series combine like resistors in parallel.
6.1 Stress and strainConcept

Removing the size of the object

Stress

The force per unit cross-sectional area: \(\sigma = \dfrac{F}{A}\), unit Pa (N m⁻²).

Strain

The extension per unit original length: \(\varepsilon = \dfrac{x}{L}\), no unit.

Young modulus

The ratio of stress to strain, within the limit of proportionality: \(E = \dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\), unit Pa.

\(E = \dfrac{F/A}{x/L} = \dfrac{FL}{Ax}\)
Fix in your notes"Stress = how big a force can affect the object" scores 0. Say force per unit cross-sectional area.
Area of a wire\(A = \dfrac{\pi d^2}{4}\). The micrometer gives the diameter; don't use it as the radius.
6.1 Stress and strainConcept

Why we bother with \(E\)

Spring constant \(k\)Young modulus \(E\)
Typeextrinsic: depends on the sample's dimensionsintrinsic: depends only on the material
UnitN m⁻¹Pa
Same material, longer wiresmallerunchanged
\(k = \dfrac{EA}{L}\)

Dividing \(F\) by \(A\) and \(x\) by \(L\) cancels out the physical dimensions, leaving a property of the material alone. A cylinder is stiffer with a larger \(E\) or larger \(A\); a longer one is easier to stretch.

Beyond the syllabusYoung modulus is one of three elastic moduli: Young (force perpendicular to the cross-section), shear (force parallel to a face) and bulk (pressure from all directions). Only Young is examined at AS.
Required practicalConcept

Measuring \(E\) for a copper wire

Wire clamped at one end, run over a pulley, masses on a hanger. A marker on the wire moves along a scale on the bench.

  1. Add a small initial load to make the wire taut and free from kinks.
  2. Measure the original length \(L\) (clamp to marker) with a metre rule. Use a long wire (over 2 m) so the extension is big enough to measure.
  3. Measure \(d\) with a micrometer screw gauge at several points and orientations; take the mean (the wire may not be uniform; averaging reduces random error).
  4. Add masses one at a time; record the marker position each time. \(F = mg\).
  5. Remove the loads and check the marker returns to zero: elastic limit not exceeded.
  6. Plot \(\Delta L\) against \(F\); gradient \(G\); \(E = \dfrac{4L}{\pi d^2 G}\).
Plot, don't substituteTake at least 5–6 readings and use the gradient of a best-fit line. One reading can't show an anomaly; a line averages out random error.
Required practicalConcept

Vertical two-wire (Searle's) method

Test wirecopper, carries the slotted masses
Reference wiresame material, hangs beside it, carries the main scale
Vernier scaleattached to the test wire, slides against the main scale
Why a reference wireAnything affecting both wires cancels out: temperature changes (thermal expansion) and the beam/support sagging or the clamps slipping. Only the load's extension is measured.
Why a vernierThe extension is small (fractions of a mm), so it needs a more precise reading than a metre rule gives.
  • Small initial tension makes both wires taut.
  • Add masses gradually so the elastic limit isn't passed.
  • Original length over 2 m ⇒ larger \(\Delta L\) ⇒ smaller percentage uncertainty.
Required practicalConcept

The graph method

Derivation (as in Exercise Q4(c)(i))

\(E = \dfrac{FL}{A\,\Delta L} = \dfrac{F}{\Delta L}\cdot\dfrac{L}{\pi d^2/4}\)

\(G = \dfrac{\Delta L}{F}\Rightarrow\dfrac{F}{\Delta L} = \dfrac1G\)

\(E = \dfrac{4L}{\pi d^2 G}\)

UnitsGraph read in mm: convert \(G\) to m N⁻¹ before substituting. 0.13 mm N⁻¹ = \(1.3\times10^{-4}\) m N⁻¹.
If F is on the y-axisThe gradient is \(k\) directly and \(E = kL/A\). Always check which way round the axes are.

6.2 Elastic and plasticConcept

Does it come back?

Elastic deformation

The object returns to its original shape and size when the load is removed.

Plastic deformation

The object does not return to its original shape when the load is removed; it is permanently deformed.

Elastic limit

The maximum load beyond which the object will not return to its original length when the load is removed.

Testing the elastic limitRemove the extra load and check the pointer returns to its original position (no permanent extension).
6.2 Elastic and plasticConcept

Three types of material

Ductileshows both elastic and plastic behaviour; large plastic region; can be drawn into wires (copper, aluminium)
Brittleelastic up to breaking; breaks suddenly with little or no plastic deformation (glass, ceramics)
Polymericelastic (recovers) but does not obey Hooke's law; curve steepens at large extension (rubber)
Mechanical propertiesStrength = force needed to break; stiffness = resistance to deformation (large \(k\) or \(E\)); ductility = can be drawn into wires; brittleness = snaps at the elastic limit.
6.2 Elastic and plasticConcept

Energy stored = area under F–x

Stretching needs work done against the restoring force; that work is stored as elastic (strain) potential energy: \(W = \Delta E\).

Derivation

Force rises from 0 to \(F\), so average force \(= \dfrac{F}{2}\)

\(W = \dfrac F2\times x = \dfrac12Fx\)

With \(F = kx\): \(E_P = \dfrac12kx^2\)

Only within the limit of proportionality\(\tfrac12kx^2\) needs a straight line. For a curve, count squares under it.
Hard ball vs soft ball (Ex Q2c)The soft ball deforms a lot and absorbs much of its KE as its own strain energy. The hard ball barely deforms, so more energy goes to the glass as strain energy ⇒ it breaks.

Example 1 · Steel wire

Q: A steel wire of length 2.50 m and diameter 0.50 mm extends by 2.5 mm under a load of 40 N. Calculate (a) the stress, (b) the strain, (c) the Young modulus, (d) the energy stored.

  1. C1 \(A = \dfrac{\pi d^2}{4} = \dfrac{\pi(0.50\times10^{-3})^2}{4} = 1.96\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2\)
  2. A1 \(\sigma = \dfrac FA = \dfrac{40}{1.96\times10^{-7}} = 2.04\times10^{8}\ \text{Pa}\)
  3. A1 \(\varepsilon = \dfrac xL = \dfrac{2.5\times10^{-3}}{2.50} = 1.0\times10^{-3}\) (no unit)
  4. A1 \(E = \dfrac\sigma\varepsilon = \dfrac{2.04\times10^8}{1.0\times10^{-3}} = 2.0\times10^{11}\ \text{Pa}\)
  5. C1A1 \(E_P = \tfrac12Fx = \tfrac12(40)(2.5\times10^{-3}) = 0.050\ \text{J}\)
Where marks goForgetting mm → m, or using \(\pi d^2\) instead of \(\pi d^2/4\) (makes \(E\) 4× too small).

Example 2 · E from a ΔL–F graph

Q: A copper wire of original length 2.20 m and mean diameter 0.38 mm is loaded. A graph of extension ΔL against force F is a straight line through (0, 0) and (6.0 N, 0.92 mm). Determine the Young modulus.

  1. C1 \(G = \dfrac{\Delta L}{F} = \dfrac{0.92\times10^{-3}}{6.0} = 1.53\times10^{-4}\ \text{m N}^{-1}\)
  2. C1 \(E = \dfrac{4L}{\pi d^2 G}\)
  3. A1 \(E = \dfrac{4(2.20)}{\pi(0.38\times10^{-3})^2(1.53\times10^{-4})} = 1.3\times10^{11}\ \text{Pa}\)
Sanity checkCopper ≈ \(1.1\)–\(1.3\times10^{11}\) Pa. An answer near \(10^{8}\) or \(10^{14}\) means a units slip.

Example 3 · One spring vs two

Q: Each spring has \(k = 200\) N m⁻¹. A 6.0 N load hangs from (a) one spring, (b) two in series, (c) two in parallel. Find the extension and the energy stored in each case.

  1. A1 One: \(x = \dfrac{6.0}{200} = 0.030\) m; \(E_P = \tfrac12(6.0)(0.030) = 0.090\) J
  2. A1 Series: \(k_{\text{eff}} = 100\) N m⁻¹; \(x = 0.060\) m; \(E_P = \tfrac12(6.0)(0.060) = 0.18\) J
  3. A1 Parallel: \(k_{\text{eff}} = 400\) N m⁻¹; \(x = 0.015\) m; \(E_P = \tfrac12(6.0)(0.015) = 0.045\) J
PatternSame load: the less stiff arrangement stretches more and stores more energy.

Example 4 · Brittle glass fibre

Q: A glass fibre, length 0.24 m, area \(7.9\times10^{-7}\) m², breaks at \(F = 60\) N with \(x = 3.0\times10^{-4}\) m (straight line throughout). Find (a) the ultimate tensile stress, (b) the Young modulus, (c) the energy stored just before it breaks.

  1. B1 Straight line to fracture ⇒ brittle
  2. C1A1 \(\sigma = \dfrac{60}{7.9\times10^{-7}} = 7.6\times10^{7}\ \text{Pa}\)
  3. C1 \(\varepsilon = \dfrac{3.0\times10^{-4}}{0.24} = 1.25\times10^{-3}\)
  4. A1 \(E = \dfrac{7.6\times10^7}{1.25\times10^{-3}} = 6.1\times10^{10}\ \text{Pa}\)
  5. C1A1 \(E_P = \tfrac12(60)(3.0\times10^{-4}) = 9.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{J}\)
MistakesConcept

Where marks are lost

mm left in mmDiameters and extensions are usually given in mm. Convert to m before using them.
Diameter as radius\(A = \pi r^2 = \pi d^2/4\). Mixing them up is a factor of 4.
Strain with a unitStrain is a ratio of two lengths: no unit.
\(Fx\) instead of \(\tfrac12Fx\)The force builds up from zero, so use the area of the triangle.
\(\tfrac12kx^2\) on a curveOnly valid up to the limit of proportionality. Beyond it, use the area under the curve.
Limit of proportionality = elastic limitNot the same point. Between them: curved but still elastic.
Vague definitions"How easy it is to deform" won't score. Use per unit wording: force per unit extension, force per unit area, extension per unit length.
Axes the wrong way roundΔL–F gradient = \(1/k\); F–ΔL gradient = \(k\). Check before you invert.

Practice · Deformation of solids

Q1 · Extension of a wire[3]

A copper wire (\(E = 1.2\times10^{11}\) Pa) of length 1.50 m and diameter 0.30 mm supports a 2.0 kg mass. Calculate the stress and the extension.

Answer\(A = 7.07\times10^{-8}\) m²; \(\sigma = 19.62/A = 2.8\times10^{8}\) Pa; \(x = \sigma L/E = 3.5\times10^{-3}\) m (3.5 mm).
Q2 · Spring energy[3]

A spring extends 5.0 cm under 4.0 N (within the limit of proportionality). Find \(k\), then the energy stored when it is stretched by 3.0 cm.

Answer\(k = 4.0/0.050 = 80\) N m⁻¹; \(E_P = \tfrac12(80)(0.030)^2 = 0.036\) J.
Q3 · Two-wire experiment[3]

In a two-wire experiment, a copper test wire of length 2.80 m and diameter 0.46 mm extends by 2.70 mm when 2.0 kg is added. Calculate \(E\) and state why a reference wire is used.

Answer\(A = 1.66\times10^{-7}\) m²; \(E = \dfrac{(19.62)(2.80)}{(1.66\times10^{-7})(2.70\times10^{-3})} = 1.2\times10^{11}\) Pa. Reference wire compensates for temperature changes and sagging of the support.
Q4 · Combinations[4]

Springs of \(k_1 = 150\) N m⁻¹ and \(k_2 = 300\) N m⁻¹ support a 9.0 N load. Find the total extension when they are (a) in series, (b) in parallel (assume the load stays level).

Answer(a) \(1/k = 1/150 + 1/300 \Rightarrow k = 100\) N m⁻¹, \(x = 0.090\) m. (b) \(k = 450\) N m⁻¹, \(x = 0.020\) m.
Q5 · Uncertainty[3]

\(d = 0.40 \pm 0.01\) mm, \(L = 2.000 \pm 0.002\) m, \(\Delta L = 0.80 \pm 0.02\) mm, \(F = 10.0 \pm 0.1\) N. Find the percentage uncertainty in \(E = 4FL/(\pi d^2\Delta L)\). Which measurement dominates?

Answer\(2(2.5\%) + 0.1\% + 2.5\% + 1.0\% = 8.6\%\). The diameter dominates (counted twice because it is squared).
Q6 · Explain[3]

A copper wire is loaded beyond its elastic limit and then unloaded. Sketch the F–x graph and explain why the wire does not return to its original length.

AnswerStraight line, curve to a maximum point, then a straight unloading line parallel to the original line reaching the x-axis at a positive extension. Beyond the elastic limit the deformation is plastic: layers of atoms have moved permanently, so a permanent extension remains.
