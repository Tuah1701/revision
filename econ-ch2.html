2.1 · DemandConcept

Demand

Definition · Demand 2.1.1

The quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices per period, ceteris paribus. "Able" makes it effective demand: desire backed by ability to pay.

Law of demand: higher price → lower quantity demanded (inverse relationship). Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demand curves.

Change in…EffectLanguage
Own priceMovement along D"Change in quantity demanded": expansion / contraction
Anything elseShift of D"Increase / decrease in demand": right / left
2.1 · Syllabus 2.1.2–2.1.3Concept

Non-price determinants of demand

1 · Related goods

Substitutes (Coke/Pepsi): coffee price ↑ → tea demand ↑. Complements (car/petrol): petrol price ↓ → car demand ↑.

2 · Income

Normal: income ↑ → demand ↑. Inferior: income ↑ → demand ↓ (bus tickets, second-hand clothes).

3 · Expected price

Expect a price rise (milk powder) → stock up now → demand shifts right, usually temporarily.

4 · Tastes

Advertising or a trend raises demand; a health scare lowers it.

5 · Government

Laws (compulsory seat belts). Higher income tax → lower disposable income → normal goods ↓, inferior ↑.

Also accepted

Population size and age structure, credit availability, seasons.

Exceptional upward-sloping demand

Veblen goods

Status goods (diamonds, luxury watches): a higher price signals prestige, so quantity demanded can rise.

Giffen goods

Highly inferior staples taking a large share of poor households' income (broken rice). A price rise makes better food unaffordable, so they buy more of the staple.

2.2 · SupplyConcept

Supply

Definition · Supply 2.2

The quantity producers are willing and able to sell at various prices per period, ceteris paribus.

Law of supply: higher price → higher quantity supplied, because production is more profitable and covers rising marginal cost. Price ↑ → expansion; price ↓ → contraction.

2.2 · Taxes & subsidiesConcept

How taxes move supply

Specific (unit) tax

A fixed amount per unit, e.g. $2. Supply shifts up by $2 at every quantity: a parallel shift.

Ad valorem tax

A percentage of price, e.g. 20% VAT. The tax per unit grows with price, so the gap widens: $5 → $6, $10 → $12, $30 → $36.

A subsidy is the specific-tax diagram in reverse: S shifts down by the subsidy.

2.4 · EquilibriumConcept

Market equilibrium

Definition · Equilibrium 2.4

The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no tendency for price to change. The market clears.

Demand Qd = a − bP · Supply Qs = c + dP · Equilibrium: Qd = Qs
2.4 · ShiftsConcept

When curves shift

One curve shifts: both P and Q move predictably. Both shift: one of P or Q is certain; the other depends on the relative size of the shifts.

MCQ shortcutQuantity certain, price uncertain → curves shift the same direction. Price certain, quantity uncertain → opposite directions.
CarefulSome handout lines compare "the increase in price" with "the increase in quantity". You can't compare them in general; it depends on the slopes. Say which variable is certain and which depends on the relative shifts.
2.3 · ElasticitiesConcept

One formula, four questions

Every elasticity answers: "If X changes by 1%, by what % does Y respond?"

\( E = \dfrac{\%\Delta \text{ in what responds}}{\%\Delta \text{ in what caused it}} \qquad \%\Delta = \dfrac{\text{new} - \text{original}}{\text{original}} \times 100 \)
Question it answersFormulaOn the diagram
PEDIf I change my price, what happens to quantity and revenue?%ΔQd ÷ %ΔPMovement along D
PESIf price rises, how much can producers raise output?%ΔQs ÷ %ΔPMovement along S
YEDIf incomes change, what happens to demand?%ΔQd ÷ %ΔYShift of D
XEDIf another good's price changes, what happens to mine?%ΔQd(A) ÷ %ΔP(B)Shift of D for A
Memory hook"Price measures pass along the curve; the others push it." For YED and XED the sign is the answer to "what type of good"; for PED and PES the size matters.
2.3 · PEDConcept

Price elasticity of demand

The responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price, ceteris paribus. Usually negative; quote the absolute value.

Same slope everywhere, but P/Q changes, so PED changes. Above the midpoint demand is elastic, below it inelastic; TR peaks at the midpoint.

Determinants of PED

  • Substitutes: the most important; more and closer → more elastic.
  • Share of income: a 10% rise on a $1,000 staycation matters far more than on $100 of bus fares.
  • Necessity vs luxury: rice inelastic, luxury watches elastic.
  • Habit / addiction: cigarettes → inelastic.
  • Time: more elastic in the long run.
  • Breadth of definition: food inelastic; one brand of bread elastic.
2.3 · PEDConcept

Applications of PED

Firms

Raise price if inelastic, cut if elastic. Advertising and branding aim to make demand more inelastic; measuring PED afterwards shows whether it worked.

Government

Tax goods with inelastic demand for revenue. Sin taxes on cigarettes and sugary drinks raise revenue but cut consumption only a little. Subsidies change consumption most when demand is elastic.

The same tax raises more revenue when demand is inelastic, because quantity barely falls. With elastic demand quantity falls a lot and producers bear most of the tax.

Evaluating PEDEstimates rely on past data; ceteris paribus rarely holds; PED differs along the curve and over time; rivals may react; firms care about profit, not just revenue. A useful guide, not a precise forecast.
2.3 · PESConcept

Price elasticity of supply

Definition · PES

The responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price, ceteris paribus. PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP. Positive.

Straight-line supply: starts on the price axis → elastic; through the origin → unitary at every point; starts on the quantity axis → inelastic. Horizontal = perfectly elastic; vertical = perfectly inelastic.

Determinants

  • Spare capacity: idle machines and workers → elastic.
  • Stocks: storable goods → elastic; perishables → inelastic.
  • Factor mobility: easy to switch resources → elastic.
  • Time: supply becomes more elastic as time passes.
  • Nature of the good: agricultural goods low PES (growing seasons).

Uses

Firms: production planning, inventory, just-in-time. Government: low farm PES causes volatile prices → buffer stocks; infrastructure, cold storage and training raise PES.

Limits

Hard to estimate; ceteris paribus; very different SR vs LR. Rubber trees take years to mature, so higher prices can't raise supply this season.

2.3 · YED & XEDConcept

Income and cross elasticity

Definition · YED

Responsiveness of demand to a change in income, ceteris paribus. YED = %ΔQd ÷ %ΔY.

YEDType
> 1Luxury (normal): demand rises more than income
0–1Necessity (normal): rises less than income
< 0Inferior: demand falls as income rises

Firms plan over the business cycle and diversify ranges (luxury, everyday, no-frills shampoo). Government taxes luxuries in booms; rising demand for inferior goods signals hardship. A good can switch category as a country gets richer.

Definition · XED

Responsiveness of demand for A to a change in the price of B. XED = %ΔQd(A) ÷ %ΔP(B). Sign = relationship; size = strength.

−∞ close complements·0 unrelated·close substitutes +∞

Main determinant: how closely the goods meet the same need (Coke is closer to Pepsi than to mineral water). Firms watch rivals' prices and bundle complements; governments predict knock-on effects (fuel tax → public transport).

2.5 · SurplusConcept

Consumer and producer surplus

Consumer surplus 2.5

The difference between the maximum price consumers are willing and able to pay and the price they actually pay.

Producer surplus

The difference between the price producers receive and the minimum they'd accept (their marginal cost).

2.4.3 · Related marketsConcept

Relationships between markets

Method: change in market A → new price in A → shift in market B → new equilibrium in B. Draw two diagrams side by side.

Substitutes

Coffee costs rise → coffee S left → coffee price ↑ → tea relatively cheaper → tea D right → tea P and Q rise.

Complements

New tech → washing machine S right → price ↓, Q ↑ → washing powder D right → its P and Q rise.

Derived demand

Factors are wanted for what they produce. Supermarket demand ↑ → demand for supermarket workers ↑ → wages and jobs rise.

Joint supply

Two goods from one process. Beef demand ↑ → more beef → leather S right → leather price ↓, quantity ↑.

2.4.4 · Functions of priceConcept

Rationing, signalling, incentive

Price mechanism 2.4.4

Demand and supply set prices, and changes in those prices allocate scarce resources between competing uses, without central direction.

FunctionAnswersActs onOn the diagram
SignallingWhatBothThe change in P itself
IncentiveHow muchMainly producersMovement along S
RationingFor whomConsumersMovement along D

Transmission of preferences: consumers "vote" with spending; the price change passes their wants to producers automatically.

Common errorThe incentive function is a movement along S (extension), not a shift. Rationing is a contraction of quantity demanded, not a fall in demand.
2.4.4 · EvaluationConcept

How the three work as one chain

Shock: Covid-19, mask demand surges→Price bid up→Signal: masks highly valued→Incentive: lines switch, new producers→Rationing: high price limits masks→Supply rises, price falls back

Explaining them as a sequence from one price change lifts an answer from listing (L1–2) to analysis (L3).

When the price mechanism fails

Worked example 1 · 2.4Worked example

Solve the equilibrium, then add a tax

Q: \(Q_d = 50 - 5P\) and \(Q_s = 2 + 3P\). Find P* and Q*. Then a $2 specific tax is imposed. Who bears it?

  1. Set \(Q_d = Q_s\): \(50 - 5P = 2 + 3P\).
  2. \(48 = 8P\), so P* = $6.
  3. \(Q^* = 50 - 5(6) = 20\). Check: \(2 + 3(6) = 20\). Q* = 20 units.
  4. The tax means producers keep \(P - 2\): new supply \(Q_s = 2 + 3(P - 2) = 3P - 4\).
  5. \(50 - 5P = 3P - 4 \Rightarrow 54 = 8P \Rightarrow P = \$6.75\); \(Q = 50 - 5(6.75) = 16.25\).
  6. Consumers pay $0.75 more; producers keep $4.75, i.e. $1.25 less. Consumers bear $0.75 of the $2, producers $1.25.
Worked example 2 · PEDWorked example

Calculate PED and use it

Q: Price rises from $12 to $18 and quantity demanded falls from 200,000 to 180,000. Calculate PED and state what happened to total revenue.

  1. %ΔP = (18 − 12) ÷ 12 × 100 = +50%.
  2. %ΔQd = (180,000 − 200,000) ÷ 200,000 × 100 = −10%.
  3. PED = −10 ÷ 50 = −0.2, so |PED| = 0.2 < 1: inelastic. A 1% price rise cuts Qd by 0.2%.
  4. TR before = 12 × 200,000 = $2.4m. TR after = 18 × 180,000 = $3.24m.
  5. TR rose by $0.84m, as expected for an inelastic good: the gain on units still sold outweighs the loss on units no longer sold.
Worked example 3 · PES & YEDWorked example

PES and YED from handout data

Q1: Price $16 → $18.40; quantity supplied 28,000 → 28,840. PES?

  1. %ΔP = 2.40 ÷ 16 = +15%. %ΔQs = 840 ÷ 28,000 = +3%.
  2. PES = 3 ÷ 15 = 0.2: inelastic. Producers can barely raise output, typical of farm goods in the short run.

Q2: Income $800 → $1,000; clothes bought 100 → 140. YED and type?

  1. %ΔY = 200 ÷ 800 = +25%. %ΔQd = 40 ÷ 100 = +40%.
  2. YED = 40 ÷ 25 = +1.6. Positive → normal; above 1 → a luxury.
Worked example 4 · XEDWorked example

XED: two examples

Q1: Coffee $10 → $12; orange juice bought 1,500 → 1,650 kg. XED?

  1. %ΔP coffee = +20%. %ΔQd juice = 150 ÷ 1,500 = +10%.
  2. XED = +10 ÷ +20 = +0.5: positive, below 1 → weak substitutes.

Q2: Pencils $1.00 → $1.30; erasers bought 1,000 → 800. XED?

  1. %ΔP pencils = +30%. %ΔQd erasers = −200 ÷ 1,000 = −20%.
  2. XED = −20 ÷ +30 ≈ −0.67: negative → complements, weak.
Handout correctionThe handout uses 400 instead of 800, getting −60% and XED = −2, then calls the goods "close substitutes". A negative XED always means complements. Example 1 also mentions tea where the table uses orange juice. Check with Puan Zaihan.
Worked example 5 · 2.5Worked example

Consumer and producer surplus

Q1: You'd pay $1.00 for one drink and $0.90 for a second. The price is $0.75. Total CS?

  1. Drink 1: 1.00 − 0.75 = $0.25. Drink 2: 0.90 − 0.75 = $0.15.
  2. Total CS = $0.40.

Q2: A firm would sell unit 1 for $2.00 and unit 2 for $2.20. Market price $3.00. PS?

  1. Unit 1: 3.00 − 2.00 = $1.00. Unit 2: 3.00 − 2.20 = $0.80.
  2. PS = $1.80.

Q3: On a linear diagram, PS is a triangle with base 8 units and height $4. PS?

  1. Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × 8 × 4 = $16.
Worked example 6 · Paper 2Worked example

Equilibrium after a wage rise [8]

Q (9708/22 M/J 2023 Q3a): Using a diagram, explain market equilibrium and consider how far a general rise in wages would change the equilibrium price and quantity of a product.

  1. AO1: define equilibrium (Qd = Qs, no tendency to change); diagram with D0, S0, E0 at P0, Q0; explain surplus above, shortage below.
  2. AO2 supply side: wages are a cost, so S shifts left to S1: price up to P1, quantity down to Q1.
  3. AO2 demand side: wages are income; for a normal good D shifts right to D1, pushing price up further to P2.
  4. AO3: price almost certainly rises. Quantity depends on relative shifts: labour-intensive haircuts → S shift dominates, Q falls; high-YED restaurant meals → D shift may dominate, Q rises; inferior goods → D falls.
  5. Conclude: price rises; how far quantity changes, and in which direction, depends on labour intensity and YED (and PED, PES).

Paper 2 · MistakesMistakes

Common mistakes that cost marks

  • "Price rises so demand falls." Write quantity demanded falls (movement).
  • Shifting demand when the question is about a cost or tax (that shifts supply).
  • Judging elasticity from slope alone across different diagrams or units.
  • Forgetting the sign: negative XED = complements; negative YED = inferior.
  • Calling the incentive function a shift of supply.
  • A conclusion that only repeats the essay. It must make a judgement.

Diagram checklist

  • Axes labelled Price and Quantity; origin O.
  • Curves D0, D1, S0, S1 with a shift arrow.
  • Equilibria dashed to both axes: P0, Q0, P1, Q1.
  • Shade and name any area (CS, PS, tax revenue, TR gain/loss).
  • Refer to it: "as shown in Figure 1, price rises from P0 to P1".
  • Inelastic curves visibly steep, elastic flat, in separate diagrams.
Data response calculationsWrite the formula, show both % changes with signs, give the answer to 2 d.p., then interpret it in words ("inelastic, so…").

Q1 · Pack Q6[1]

Supply of a good falls and its price rises. What is the only certain outcome on the demand side?

ContractionA fall in quantity demanded: a movement up along D. Demand itself hasn't changed.
Q2 · Pack Q23[1]

A tax on cigarettes cuts restaurant meal sales. What happens in each market?

AnswerCigarettes: the tax shifts S, so a movement along D. Meals (a complement for smokers): demand shifts left.
Q3 · Pack Q26[1]

The government advises people to drink less fizzy drink. Effect on the sugar market?

AnswerFizzy drink D shifts left. Sugar demand is derived from it, so sugar demand falls: a movement down the sugar supply curve.
Q4 · Pack Q1[1]

A car park has a fixed number of spaces and demand varies by day. How can price clear the market each day?

Vary the priceSupply is fixed (vertical S), so charge a different price each day where that day's demand meets the fixed supply.
Q5 · Calculation[2]

A firm's PED is 0.4. It raises price by 10%. By how much does quantity demanded change, and what happens to revenue?

−4%; TR rises%ΔQd = 0.4 × 10 = 4% fall. Inelastic, so the price rise increases total revenue.

Practice · Paper 2 essays

Q6 · PED[8]

Explain, using a diagram, how a firm can use PED to decide its pricing, and consider whether raising price will always raise its revenue.

PlanDefine PED + formula; TR gain/loss diagrams for inelastic vs elastic. Consider: only if demand is inelastic at the current price; PED changes along the curve; rivals may not follow; revenue isn't profit. Conclude: not always.
Q7 · YED[8]

Explain the difference between a normal and an inferior good using YED, and consider whether a producer of luxury goods should worry about a recession.

PlanYED formula; + vs −; luxury > 1. Hotel with YED +2.5: incomes −5% → demand ≈ −12.5%. Consider: depends on its customers' incomes (the very rich may be little affected), brand strength, and whether it can add budget lines.
Q8 · Price mechanism[12]

Assess whether the price mechanism is the best way to allocate a staple food, such as rice, after a sudden fall in supply.

PlanFor: S left → price ↑ → rationing, signal to farmers and importers, incentive over time; no bureaucracy. Against: rice is a necessity with low PED, a big share of poor budgets → harsh rationing by ability to pay; low PES → slow response. Alternatives: maximum price (blocks the signal), buffer stock release, transfers, imports. Judge: short run, targeted transfers or stock release; long run, let price signal more supply.
Q9 · Elasticity[12]

Assess whether PED is more useful than YED to a firm.

PlanPED: price the firm controls → TR decision now. YED: plan output over the cycle (car maker YED +2, incomes −3% → demand ≈ −6%). Evaluate: PED wins in the short run with price-setting power; YED wins for luxury or inferior goods over the cycle; both rely on past data. Judge: PED for most firms, with that exception.
