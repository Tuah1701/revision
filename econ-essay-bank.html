1. Basic economic ideas and resource allocation

ASScarcity · PPC · factors · systems · goods

Scarcity and opportunity cost sit underneath every essay in the course. If you can define them precisely and draw the PPC, you have an intro and a diagram for half the paper.

1Definitions to learn word for word

Scarcity

Unlimited wants but finite resources, so not every want can be satisfied and choices must be made.

Opportunity cost

The value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.

Production possibility curve

The maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all resources are fully and efficiently employed, with given technology.

Market economy

Resources allocated by the price mechanism, with private ownership and minimal government intervention.

Planned economy

The state decides what, how and for whom to produce; resources are mostly state-owned.

Mixed economy

Resources allocated partly by the price mechanism (private sector) and partly by the state (public sector).

Public good

Non-excludable and non-rival, so consumers can free ride; no effective demand is revealed, so the market provides none.

Merit good

A good the free market under-consumes because consumers underestimate its benefits (information failure); it often has external benefits.

2Analysis chains to recycle

Price mechanism

Demand rises→Shortage at old price→Price rises→Signal + profit incentive→Resources move to that good→Higher price rations what's left

Investment and the PPC

More capital goods now→Fewer consumer goods (opportunity cost)→Movement along PPC→Capital stock grows→PPC shifts outward later

Public goods

Non-excludable→Free-rider problem→No effective demand revealed→Firms can't charge→Missing market → state provides

Planned economy weakness

No price signals→Planners must forecast demand→Errors → shortages and surpluses→No profit incentive→Productive inefficiency (inside PPC)

3Diagrams to have ready

DiagramUse it for
PPC: movement alongOpportunity cost of a choice; reallocating resources
PPC: point insideUnemployment, inefficiency, recession
PPC: outward shiftGrowth, investment, education, technology
Concave PPCIncreasing opportunity cost (resources are specialised)

4Evaluation you can reuse

It depends on…Sentence to adapt
Time periodIn the short run investment costs consumer goods, but in the long run it shifts the PPC outward, so consumers gain too.
Degree of market failureMarkets work well for private goods in competitive markets, but fail badly for public and merit goods.
Government failureState allocation only improves on the market if the government has good information, the right incentives and little corruption.
Value judgementWhether an allocation is 'desirable' depends on whether society puts more weight on efficiency or on equity.

5Examples to drop in

Malaysia: mixed economy

Private firms produce most goods; the state runs Klinik Kesihatan, schools, and holds stakes in GLCs such as Petronas and TNB.

Public goods

Street lighting, national defence, flood-mitigation schemes.

Merit goods

Vaccinations, education, health screenings.

Point inside the PPC

The 2020 Movement Control Order left labour and capital idle.

Marks students lose here
  • Saying public goods aren't provided because 'there's no profit'. Use the free-rider problem.
  • Labelling PPC axes X and Y. Use the goods named in the question (or 'capital goods' and 'consumer goods').
  • Confusing a movement along the PPC (reallocation) with a shift (change in capacity).

Why the PPC bows outwards

8 marksPaper 2 · Sect B~16 minPast-paper style

Explain why a production possibility curve is usually drawn concave to the origin and consider whether an economy will always produce on its production possibility curve.

Key points the examiner wants

  • AO1Define the PPC: maximum output combinations with resources fully and efficiently employed.
  • AO1Accurate diagram: concave PPC, named axes, points showing a reallocation.
  • AO2Resources are not equally suited to producing both goods (specialised).
  • AO2So each extra unit of one good costs increasing amounts of the other: increasing opportunity cost.
  • AO3Consider: points inside the PPC from unemployment or inefficiency; trade lets consumption go outside.
  • AO3Conclusion answering 'always': no.

Two-minute plan

Define PPC→Diagram A→B→C→Specialised resources → rising opportunity cost→Consider: inside (unemployment, inefficiency)→Verdict: not always
Your attempt, then the model[8]

Write it first (paper, or the pencil space), timing yourself to ~16 min. Then reveal the model and compare the highlighted points.

Model answer

DefineA production possibility curve (PPC) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently employed, given current technology.

ExplainThe PPC is usually concave because resources are not equally suited to producing both goods. In the figure, moving from A to B, the first resources switched to consumer goods are those best suited to them, so little capital output is lost. Moving from B to C, the economy must now use resources such as engineers and specialist machinery that are much better at producing capital goods, so each extra unit of consumer goods costs more capital goods. This increasing opportunity cost makes the curve bow outwards. If all resources were equally suited, the opportunity cost would be constant and the PPC would be a straight line.

ConsiderHowever, an economy will not always produce on its PPC. If there is unemployment, as when Malaysia's 2020 Movement Control Order left labour and factories idle, it produces at a point inside the curve such as U. Output can also be inside the PPC because of productive inefficiency, for example when state firms in a planned economy lack a profit incentive to minimise costs. Points outside such as X cannot be produced, although trade allows a country to consume outside its PPC.

ConclusionSo the PPC is concave because of increasing opportunity cost, but it shows potential output. In practice most economies produce slightly inside it, because some unemployment and inefficiency always exist.

Why this scoresThe diagram and definition secure AO1 quickly. The key AO2 step is why opportunity cost rises (specialised resources), not just that it does. 'Consider' is answered with two distinct reasons for being inside, then a one-line verdict on 'always'.
Topic 1 · AS · Essay 2Model essay

Is a mixed economy always best?

12 marksPaper 2 · Sect B~24 minPast-paper style

Assess whether a mixed economy is always the best way to allocate scarce resources.

Key points the examiner wants

  • AO1Define scarcity and the three questions (what, how, for whom); define a mixed economy.
  • AO2Market side: the price mechanism signals, gives incentives and rations, leading to efficiency and consumer sovereignty.
  • AO2Government side corrects market failure: free-rider problem for public goods, under-consumption of merit goods, inequality.
  • AO2Compare with a planned economy: no price signals, weak incentives, shortages.
  • AO3Against: government failure, distorting subsidies, inefficient state firms; 'best' depends on the balance and quality of government.
  • AO3Justified conclusion: usually best, but not always.

Two-minute plan

Define scarcity + mixed economy→For: market efficiency→For: state fixes public/merit goods→Against: government failure, wrong balance→Depends on: quality of government→Verdict: usually, not always
Your attempt, then the model[12]

Write it first (paper, or the pencil space), timing yourself to ~24 min. Then reveal the model and compare the highlighted points.

Model answer

IntroBecause of scarcity, every economy must decide what to produce, how and for whom. In a mixed economy, such as Malaysia's, these decisions are made partly by the price mechanism and partly by the government. Whether this is always best depends on how well each sector does its job.

For: marketsA mixed economy keeps the price mechanism for most private goods. When demand for a good rises, its price rises, which signals to producers that consumers want more and gives them a profit incentive to move resources into it, while the higher price rations the limited supply. Competition pushes firms to cut costs, so resources are used efficiently and output follows consumer preferences (consumer sovereignty). A planned economy lacks these signals, so planners must guess demand, which tends to cause shortages and surpluses.

For: governmentThe government sector corrects what markets get wrong. Public goods such as street lighting are non-excludable, so people can free ride; no effective demand is revealed and the market provides none. Merit goods such as vaccinations are under-consumed because people underestimate their benefits. By providing these, and by using taxes and transfers to reduce inequality, a mixed economy achieves outcomes neither a pure market nor pure planning would.

AgainstHowever, a mixed economy is only as good as its government. Intervention can cause government failure: blanket fuel subsidies, for example, kept petrol cheap for rich and poor alike, encouraged over-consumption and smuggling, and cost billions that could have funded schools. State-linked firms protected from competition may be productively inefficient. If the balance tilts too far towards the state, the mixed economy can suffer the same problems as a planned one.

Depends onSo 'best' depends on the balance and the quality of government. Economies with very different mixes, from lightly regulated Singapore to high-tax Nordic countries, have all done well, because their governments intervened where markets fail and left the rest to markets.

ConclusionOverall, a mixed economy is usually the best way to allocate resources because it combines the efficiency of markets with correction of market failure. However, it is not always best: where government failure is serious, a greater role for the market is likely to allocate resources better.

369 words · about 17 min of handwriting at 22 words/min, plus planning

Why this scoresBoth sides are developed as chains (signal → incentive → reallocation; non-excludable → free ride → missing market), which secures Level 3. The 'against' side isn't just 'governments are bad': it names a specific failure and its cost. The conclusion answers 'always' directly and says when the answer would change.

2. The price system and the microeconomy

ASDemand · supply · elasticities · equilibrium · surplus

Elasticity is the most recyclable idea in AS Economics. It decides tax incidence, revenue, how much a price changes after a shock, and it's the go-to 'it depends on' in almost every micro essay.

1Definitions to learn word for word

Effective demand

The quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price, ceteris paribus.

PED

The responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price, ceteris paribus. %ΔQd ÷ %ΔP.

YED

The responsiveness of demand to a change in income. Positive = normal good (0–1 necessity, >1 luxury); negative = inferior.

XED

The responsiveness of demand for good A to a change in the price of good B. Positive = substitutes, negative = complements.

PES

The responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price. %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP.

Consumer surplus

The difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay.

2Analysis chains to recycle

Revenue and PED

Demand elastic→Price cut → proportionately larger rise in Qd→TR rises→(Inelastic: raise price to raise TR)

Tax incidence

Indirect tax shifts S up→If demand inelastic→Consumers can't switch→Most of the tax is passed on as a higher price

PES and time

Price rises→Short run: fixed capacity, crops take time→Qs barely rises (inelastic)→Long run: new capacity, planting→Qs rises more (elastic)

Price after a supply shock

Supply falls→Shortage at old price→Price rises→How much depends on PED

3Diagrams to have ready

DiagramUse it for
D/S shiftAny shock: new equilibrium price and quantity
TR rectanglesPricing decisions and PED
CS / PSWelfare effects of a price change
Steep vs flat curveInelastic vs elastic demand or supply

4Evaluation you can reuse

It depends on…Sentence to adapt
Accuracy of estimatesElasticity values are estimated from past data when other factors were also changing, so they may be unreliable guides to the future.
Time periodDemand and supply are more elastic in the long run, as buyers find substitutes and firms expand capacity.
Position on the curvePED is not constant along a straight-line demand curve, so a price change may push the good into a different elasticity range.
Rival reactionsA firm's own price elasticity depends on whether competitors match its price change.

5Examples to drop in

Inelastic demand

RON95 petrol, rice, cigarettes: few substitutes, necessities or habit-forming.

Elastic demand

A single brand of mass-market car or smartphone with many close substitutes.

YED

Luxury cars YED >1; budget models and buses may be inferior as incomes rise.

Inelastic supply

Durians and palm oil in the short run: trees take years to mature.

Marks students lose here
  • Writing 'price rises so demand falls'. It's quantity demanded (a movement), not demand (a shift).
  • Forgetting 'ceteris paribus' in an elasticity definition.
  • Saying inelastic means 'consumers don't respond at all'. That's perfectly inelastic (PED = 0).
Topic 2 · AS · Essay 1Model essay

Why is farm supply inelastic?

8 marksPaper 2 · Sect B~16 minPast-paper style

Explain the factors that determine the price elasticity of supply of a product and consider why the price elasticity of supply of agricultural products such as durians is often lower than that of manufactured goods.

Key points the examiner wants

  • AO1Define PES with the formula: %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP; elastic >1, inelastic <1.
  • AO2Determinants: spare capacity, stocks, time period, factor mobility, length of the production period.
  • AO2Each explained as a chain: why it lets firms raise output quickly or not.
  • AO3Consider: durians have a long growing period, are perishable (no stocks) and depend on weather.
  • AO3Conclusion: lower in the short run, but the gap narrows in the long run.

Two-minute plan

Define PES→Capacity, stocks, time, factor mobility→Consider: durians vs factory goods→Verdict: short run yes, long run less so
Your attempt, then the model[8]

Write it first (paper, or the pencil space), timing yourself to ~16 min. Then reveal the model and compare the highlighted points.

Model answer

DefinePrice elasticity of supply (PES) measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price, ceteris paribus, calculated as %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP. Supply is elastic if PES is greater than 1 and inelastic if it is less than 1.

FactorsPES depends on how easily firms can raise output when price rises. If firms have spare capacity, they can increase production quickly by using idle machines and workers, so supply is elastic. If they hold stocks of finished goods, they can release them onto the market immediately. Time matters: in the short run at least one factor is fixed, but in the long run firms can build new factories, so supply becomes more elastic. Finally, if factors of production are mobile and can easily be switched from another product, output can respond faster.

ConsiderAgricultural products like durians usually have lower PES. A durian tree takes several years to bear fruit, so the production period is long and farmers cannot respond to a higher price this season. Durians are perishable, so no stocks can be held, and output depends on weather, not just farmers' decisions. A factory making electronics, by contrast, can add a shift or draw on inventory within weeks.

ConclusionSo durian supply is much less elastic than manufactured goods in the short run. In the long run, however, farmers can plant more trees and cold storage can extend shelf life, so the gap narrows over time.

Why this scoresFour determinants, each with a 'so…' explaining the mechanism, is enough for full AO2. The 'consider' part applies three of them specifically to durians rather than repeating the list, and the conclusion adds time as the qualifier.
Topic 2 · AS · Essay 2Model essay

PED or YED for a car firm?

12 marksPaper 2 · Sect B~24 minYour teacher's sample, rewritten to exam length

Assess whether price elasticity of demand (PED) or income elasticity of demand (YED) is likely to be of greater importance to a firm producing cars.

Key points the examiner wants

  • AO1Define PED and YED precisely, with ceteris paribus.
  • AO1Diagram: TR rectangles for elastic and inelastic demand.
  • AO2PED → pricing and total revenue: elastic → cut price; inelastic → raise price; passing on cost increases.
  • AO2YED → cars are normal goods, luxury cars YED > 1 → sales swing with the business cycle → output, capacity and product-mix planning.
  • AO3Weigh: firm controls price but cannot control income; rivals react in an oligopoly; estimates are unreliable.
  • AO3Judgement depends on time horizon and segment (mass-market vs luxury).

Two-minute plan

Define PED + YED→PED: pricing, TR, cost pass-through→YED: business cycle, planning, product mix→Weigh: control, rivals, data→Verdict: PED short run, YED long run / luxury
Your attempt, then the model[12]

Write it first (paper, or the pencil space), timing yourself to ~24 min. Then reveal the model and compare the highlighted points.

Model answer

IntroPED measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price, ceteris paribus, while YED measures the responsiveness of demand to a change in consumers' income. Which matters more to a car producer depends on the time horizon and the type of car it sells.

PEDPED is important because it tells the firm how a price change will affect total revenue. A mass-market brand such as Proton competes with close substitutes like Perodua and Toyota, so its demand is likely to be price elastic: a price cut causes a proportionately larger rise in quantity demanded, so revenue rises, as shown by the gain area exceeding the loss area in the figure. A luxury brand with loyal buyers has more inelastic demand, so it can raise price and increase revenue. PED also tells the firm how much of a cost increase, such as dearer steel or a higher sales tax, it can pass on without losing many sales.

YEDYED matters because cars are normal goods with high positive YED, and luxury cars may have YED greater than 1. Cars are expensive and often bought on credit, so when incomes rise in a boom, demand rises more than proportionately, and in a recession sales collapse. Knowing YED helps the firm plan output, capacity and stock levels for the business cycle, and to diversify its product range, for example adding cheaper models whose sales hold up better when incomes fall.

WeighingPED is more immediately useful because price is a variable the firm controls, while it cannot control incomes and must rely on uncertain forecasts. However, the car market is an oligopoly, so if rivals match a price cut, the firm's demand is far less elastic than it estimated. Income changes, in contrast, shift the whole demand curve, and are often the largest source of swings in car sales. Both measures assume ceteris paribus, while car demand also depends on interest rates and government incentives such as tax exemptions for electric vehicles.

ConclusionOverall, PED is likely to be of greater importance for a mass-market car firm in the short run, because it directly guides pricing and revenue. However, YED is more important for long-run planning, and especially for luxury producers whose sales depend on the economic cycle, so the answer depends on the segment the firm competes in.

3. Government microeconomic intervention

ASPrice controls · taxes · subsidies · provision · inequality

Every intervention essay follows the same pattern: what market failure it fixes, the diagram, who gains and loses, and why it might not work. Learn that pattern once and reuse it for taxes, subsidies, price controls and direct provision.

1Definitions to learn word for word

Maximum price

A legal price ceiling set below equilibrium to make a good more affordable.

Minimum price

A legal price floor set above equilibrium to raise producers' incomes or reduce consumption.

Indirect tax

A tax on spending. Specific: fixed amount per unit (parallel shift). Ad valorem: percentage of price (pivot).

Subsidy

A payment from government to producers that lowers their costs, shifting supply to the right.

Incidence of a tax

How the burden of a tax is shared between consumers and producers; depends on the relative elasticities.

Transfer payment

Money paid by government with no good or service in return, e.g. cash aid to low-income households.

2Analysis chains to recycle

Maximum price

Set below equilibrium→Qd rises, Qs falls→Shortage→Queues, rationing, black markets

Indirect tax on a demerit good

Cost per unit rises→Supply shifts left→Price rises→Qd contracts→Consumption falls towards the social optimum

Subsidy on a merit good

Producers' costs fall→Supply shifts right→Price falls→Consumption rises→Opportunity cost for government

Direct provision

State funds the good from tax→Free or low price at point of use→Consumption rises→Needs funding + efficient delivery

3Diagrams to have ready

DiagramUse it for
Max / min priceShortage or surplus, black markets
Indirect tax with incidence shadedSin taxes, sugar tax, fuel tax
SubsidyMerit goods, farm support, cost to government

4Evaluation you can reuse

It depends on…Sentence to adapt
PEDHow much consumption falls after a tax depends on PED: with inelastic demand, price rises but quantity barely falls.
InformationThe government needs to know the size of the market failure to set the right tax, subsidy or price, and it rarely does.
EnforcementPrice controls need enforcement; without it, black markets develop.
Opportunity costMoney spent on subsidies cannot be spent on schools or hospitals.
EquityIndirect taxes are regressive: low-income households pay a larger share of their income.

5Examples to drop in

Price ceilings

Malaysia's festive-season maximum price scheme on items like chicken and eggs.

Sin taxes

Excise duty on sugary drinks (since 2019) and cigarettes.

Subsidies

Fuel, rice and cooking-oil subsidies; the shift to targeted RON95 subsidies.

Direct provision

Klinik Kesihatan, public schools, free vaccinations.

Marks students lose here
  • Drawing a maximum price above equilibrium. It has no effect there.
  • Not shading or naming the tax incidence areas you talk about.
  • Forgetting the cost to government (opportunity cost) of subsidies and provision.
Topic 3 · AS · Essay 1Model essay

Do price ceilings help the poor?

8 marksPaper 2 · Sect B~16 minPast-paper style

Explain the effects of a maximum price set below the equilibrium price in the market for a staple food and consider whether it benefits low-income households.

Key points the examiner wants

  • AO1Define a maximum price; diagram with Pmax below Pe, Qs and Qd marked.
  • AO2Qd rises, Qs falls → shortage (excess demand).
  • AO2Consequences: queues, rationing by first-come or favouritism, black markets.
  • AO3Consider: helps those who get the good, but lower supply means some get none; black-market price may exceed Pe.
  • AO3Conclusion: helps only if combined with supply measures or used briefly.

Two-minute plan

Define + diagram→Shortage: Qd > Qs→Queues, black market→Consider: winners vs losers among the poor→Verdict: short-term only
Your attempt, then the model[8]

Write it first (paper, or the pencil space), timing yourself to ~16 min. Then reveal the model and compare the highlighted points.

Model answer

DefineA maximum price is a legal ceiling on the price of a good. To have any effect it must be set below the equilibrium price, as at Pmax in the figure.

EffectsAt Pmax, the lower price makes the food more affordable, so quantity demanded rises from Qe to Qd. But producers receive less per unit, so less profitable farmers cut output and quantity supplied falls to Qs. The result is a shortage of Qd − Qs. Because price can no longer ration the good, it is rationed in other ways: queues, sellers favouring regular customers, or limits per buyer. Some buyers will pay more than Pmax, so a black market may develop, where the good sells illegally at prices up to Pbm.

ConsiderLow-income households who manage to buy at Pmax clearly benefit, because a staple takes a large share of their income. However, total supply has fallen, so some poor households get nothing, and those without time to queue may have to pay the black-market price, which is higher than the original equilibrium. In the long run, lower farm incomes may reduce investment, making shortages worse.

ConclusionSo a maximum price helps low-income households only partly and only in the short term, such as Malaysia's festive-season ceilings. It works better alongside a subsidy to producers that keeps supply from falling.

Topic 3 · AS · Essay 2Model essay

Is a tax the best way to cut sugar?

12 marksPaper 2 · Sect B~24 minPast-paper style

Assess whether an indirect tax is the most effective way for a government to reduce the consumption of sugary drinks.

Key points the examiner wants

  • AO1Define indirect tax; sugary drinks as a demerit good; diagram with incidence.
  • AO2Tax → supply shifts left → price rises → Qd contracts; also raises revenue; makes firms reformulate.
  • AO2Alternatives: information campaigns, regulation (limits, labelling, school bans), subsidising healthy options.
  • AO3Effectiveness depends on PED: habit-forming → inelastic → small fall in quantity.
  • AO3Regressive; needs the right rate; substitution to untaxed sugar.
  • AO3Verdict: most effective as part of a policy mix.

Two-minute plan

Define demerit good + tax→For: price signal, revenue, reformulation→Against: inelastic demand, regressive, substitution→Compare: information, regulation→Verdict: best in a mix
Your attempt, then the model[12]

Write it first (paper, or the pencil space), timing yourself to ~24 min. Then reveal the model and compare the highlighted points.

Model answer

IntroSugary drinks are a demerit good: consumers underestimate the harm to their health, so they are over-consumed in a free market. An indirect tax is a tax on spending, such as Malaysia's excise duty on sugary drinks introduced in 2019. Whether it is the most effective tool depends largely on how consumers respond to price.

ForA tax raises producers' costs per unit, so supply shifts left by the amount of the tax. The equilibrium price rises from P0 to P1, so quantity demanded contracts from Q0 to Q1. Consumers now pay closer to the full cost of the drinks, so consumption moves towards the socially optimal level. The tax also raises revenue, which can fund health campaigns or clinics, and it gives firms an incentive to reformulate drinks with less sugar to avoid the tax.

AgainstHowever, demand for sugary drinks is likely to be price inelastic because they are habit-forming and cheap relative to income. As the figure shows, most of the tax is passed on to consumers as a higher price, but quantity falls only a little, so the tax raises revenue more than it changes behaviour. The tax is also regressive, taking a bigger share of income from poorer households, and consumers may switch to untaxed sugary foods or homemade drinks.

AlternativesOther policies attack the cause directly. Information campaigns and clear labelling correct the information failure behind over-consumption and may change habits permanently. Regulation, such as banning sugary drinks in school canteens, reduces consumption among children with certainty, though it is hard to enforce widely and removes choice.

ConclusionOverall, an indirect tax is not the most effective policy on its own, because inelastic demand means consumption falls only slightly. However, it is a useful part of a policy mix: the tax revenue can fund education and labelling, which make demand more elastic over time, so the tax becomes more effective the longer it is combined with them.

4. The macroeconomy

ASNational income · AD/AS · growth · unemployment · inflation

AD/AS is the macro equivalent of demand and supply: almost every macro essay needs it. The shape of the AS curve is your strongest 'it depends on' in this whole topic.

1Definitions to learn word for word

Aggregate demand

Total planned spending on an economy's goods and services at each price level: AD = C + I + G + (X − M).

Aggregate supply

Total output producers are willing and able to supply at each price level.

Inflation

A sustained rise in the general price level, measured by a price index such as the CPI.

Demand-pull inflation

Inflation caused by AD rising faster than the economy's capacity to produce.

Cost-push inflation

Inflation caused by rising costs of production (wages, imported raw materials, energy) shifting AS left.

Unemployment

People of working age who are willing and able to work and actively seeking work but without a job.

Real GDP

GDP adjusted for inflation, so it measures changes in the volume of output.

2Analysis chains to recycle

AD rise with spare capacity

AD rises→Firms use idle resources→Output and employment rise→Little pressure on prices

AD rise near capacity

AD rises→Bottlenecks, labour shortages→Costs and prices rise→Demand-pull inflation

Cost-push

Oil or import prices rise→Firms' costs rise→AS shifts left→Price level up AND output down (stagflation)

Inflation → competitiveness

Domestic prices rise faster than rivals'→Exports dearer, imports cheaper→X falls, M rises→Current account worsens

3Diagrams to have ready

DiagramUse it for
Keynesian AS (three ranges)Whether higher AD causes output or inflation
AD shiftDemand-pull inflation, growth from spending
SRAS shift leftCost-push inflation, stagflation
LRAS shift rightLong-run growth, supply-side improvements

Spare capacityThe effect of higher AD depends on where the economy is on its AS curve: with high unemployment, output rises with little inflation.

Anticipated or notAnticipated inflation does less harm, because wages and contracts adjust.

In a customs union with a common external tariff, a good from a low-cost producer outside the bloc becomes dearer than the same good from a higher-cost member that pays no tariff. Consumers may pay slightly less than before, but the world's resources are used less efficiently.

ConsiderSo joining benefits a country only if trade creation outweighs trade diversion, which is more likely when members are already efficient producers. There are also dynamic gains: a larger market allows economies of scale, more competition and more FDI from firms wanting access to the whole bloc. Against this, the country may lose freedom to set its own trade policy.

ConclusionSo joining a trade bloc does not always benefit a country: it depends on whether trade creation and dynamic gains outweigh trade diversion.

Model answer

IntroForeign direct investment occurs when a multinational company sets up or acquires productive capacity in another country. Developing economies often lack domestic savings and capital, so FDI can fill this savings gap. Whether it is always beneficial depends on how much of the gain stays in the host country.

BenefitsFDI is an injection into the circular flow: building a factory raises aggregate demand, and through the multiplier raises national income and creates jobs beyond the plant itself. In the long run it adds to the capital stock, shifting LRAS to the right. MNCs bring technology, management skills and training; when workers move to local firms or start their own, these spread through the economy. Penang's semiconductor industry grew this way: MNCs such as Intel trained engineers who later built local supplier firms.

BenefitsMNCs often produce for export, earning foreign currency and improving the current account, and their profits and workers' incomes provide tax revenue for health and education.

CostsHowever, the benefits are not guaranteed. MNCs repatriate profits to their home country, which appears as an outflow on the current account and limits the income gain. Large foreign firms may crowd out local competitors. Many are footloose and can relocate to a lower-cost country, causing sudden unemployment. Some FDI offers only low-skill assembly jobs with little technology transfer, and weak regulation can allow environmental damage; for example, new data centres use large amounts of water and electricity. MNCs can also use transfer pricing to declare profits in low-tax countries.

Depends onSo the outcome depends on linkages: FDI benefits most when MNCs buy inputs from local suppliers. It also depends on government policy: local-content requirements, skills programmes and environmental rules keep more of the gain at home, while very generous tax holidays used to attract MNCs can give much of it away. High-technology FDI usually brings more lasting benefits than low-wage assembly.

ConclusionOverall, FDI is not always beneficial for a developing economy. It is most beneficial when it creates linkages with local firms and the government has the policies to capture skills and tax revenue, as in Penang; it is least beneficial when it is footloose, low-skilled and lightly regulated.
