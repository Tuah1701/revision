StartOverview

Definitions are free marks, if the wording is exact

A "define" or "state" question usually has 1–2 marks for one or two key phrases. Vague words score zero.

1 · Say what it is (quantity / ratio / rate)→2 · Key phrase ("per unit", "rate of change of")→3 · Condition (e.g. "limit of proportionality not exceeded")
Method tonightCover the definition, say it out loud, check it word for word. Use the Cards button (top right) to drill, and put misses in the "Again" pile.
Words that lose marks"amount" instead of "rate of change", "distance" instead of "perpendicular distance", leaving out "positive" (field strength) or "original" (stress).

Ch 2Definitions

Chapter 2: Measurement techniques

Systematic errorCh 2

Readings are consistently being either above or below true value

Random errorCh 2

Readings are scattered around true value

AccuracyCh 2

The degree to which the values obtained approach true value and is influenced by size of systematic error

Precisioncorrected to mark scheme

How close repeated readings are to each other; determined by the size of random error

UncertaintyCh 2

The total range of values within which the measurement is likely to lie

PrecisionCambridge treats precision as how close repeated readings are to each other (small random error, small spread). Accuracy = how close to the true value (small systematic error).

Ch 4Definitions

Chapter 4: Dynamics

Newton's First LawCh 4

Every body continues in a state of rest, or with uniform velocity, unless acted on by an external force.

Newton's Second LawCh 4

For a body of a constant mass, the resultant force is proportional to the rate of change of its momentum.

Newton's Third LawCh 4

When two bodies interact, the forces they exert on each other are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

Conservation of MomentumCh 4

In any closed system, the total momentum of the system remains constant given there is no external force.

ForceCh 4

Rate of change of momentum

MomentumCh 4

The product of its mass and its velocity

WeightCh 4

The force of gravity on a mass of object

Elastic collisionCh 4

Total kinetic energy before = after collision

Newton's 2nd law"Resultant force is proportional to the rate of change of momentum and acts in the direction of the change in momentum." The direction part is often the second mark.

Ch 6Definitions

Chapter 6: Work, energy and power

WorkCh 6

The product of force and the distance moved in the direction of the force

Principle of Conservation of EnergyCh 6

Energy can neither be created nor destroyed. It can only be converted from one form to another.

Gravitational Potential EnergyCh 6

The energy an object has due to its height (position) in a gravitational field.

Kinetic EnergyCh 6

Energy an object has due to its motion (or velocity).

Elastic Potential EnergyCh 6

The energy stored within a material (e.g. in a spring) when it is stretched or compressed.

Electrostatic potential energyCh 6

Energy stored due to position of a charge in an electric field

PowerCh 6

Rate of doing work

EfficiencyCh 6

Useful energy output per total energy input

GPE / KE (lost 2 marks in Set 3)Keyword pattern: energy stored in ___ due to ___. GPE: energy a mass has due to its position in a gravitational field. KE: energy a mass has due to its motion. Work: force × distance moved in the direction of the force.
Keyword (from the sheet)Potential energy: energy stored in ____ due to ______.

The ability of object to change its shape and hence does not return to its original shape after applied force is removed

Elastic DeformationCh 9

The ability of object to change its shape and hence return to its original shape after applied force is removed

StressCh 9

Force per unit original cross sectional area

StrainCh 9

The ratio of extension to its original length

Young's ModulusCh 9

The ratio of stress over strain given that limit of proportionality is not exceeded.

Ch 15Definitions

Chapter 15: Superposition (1/2)

Principle of Superposition of WavesCh 15

When two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement is equal to the sum of the individual displacement of the waves.

Coherent SourceCh 15

Waves of same frequency and constant phase difference.

CoherenceCh 15

Two or more waves that originates from a source which have constant phase difference and same frequency and wavelength.

DiffractionCh 15

The spreading or bending of waves when passing through a gap or rounding an obstacle

NodeCh 15

The point with no displacement as a result of destructive interference

AntinodeCh 15

The point with maximum displacement as a result of constructive interference.

Ch 15Definitions

Chapter 15: Superposition (2/2)

Constructive interferenceCh 15

When two waves with maximum displacement meet, resulting in a wave with amplitude of sum of the amplitudes of the individual waves

Destructive interferenceCh 15

When two waves with maximum and minimum displacement meet, resulting in a wave with zero displacement.

Condition of interferenceCh 15

Same types of waves · meet at a point · same direction of polarisation

CoherentCoherent = constant phase difference (and so the same frequency). For observable two-source interference also need similar amplitudes.

Ch 19Definitions

Chapter 19: Current of electricity

Electric CurrentCh 19

The rate of flow of electric charge

Ohm's LawCh 19

The current in a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends, provided its temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.

ResistanceCh 19

The ratio of the potential difference across a conductor to the current flowing through it

Potential differenceCh 19

Energy transferred from electrical energy to other forms of energy per unit charge from one point to another point.

Electromotive ForceCh 19

Energy transferred from other forms of energy to electrical energy per unit charge to move around a complete circuit.

SemiconductorCh 19

Materials which have intermediate electrical conductivity which varies substantially with temperature

Electrical powerblank on sheet · added

The rate of electrical energy transfer (work done per unit time): P = VI.

p.d. vs e.m.f.p.d.: electrical → other forms, per unit charge. e.m.f.: other forms → electrical, per unit charge. Resistance = p.d. / current, NOT "gradient of the I–V graph".
Ch 20Definitions

Chapter 20: D.C. circuits

Kirchhoff's First LawCh 20

The sum of the current entering a junction is equal to the sum of the current leaving it.

Kirchhoff's Second LawCh 20

The sum of electromotive force is equal to the sum of potential difference for any closed loop in a circuit.

What the laws conserveKirchhoff 1 = conservation of charge. Kirchhoff 2 = conservation of energy.

Worked exampleWorked example

Define the torque of a couple [2]

Q: Define the torque of a couple.

  1. B1: one of the forces (not "the sum of the forces")
  2. B1: × the perpendicular distance between the lines of action of the forces
  3. Full answer: The torque of a couple is the product of one of the forces and the perpendicular distance between their lines of action.
Where marks go"Force × distance" alone scores 0: no "one of", no "perpendicular".
Worked exampleWorked example

Distinguish between e.m.f. and p.d. [2]

Q: Distinguish between the electromotive force of a source and the potential difference across a resistor.

  1. B1 (e.m.f.): energy transferred from other forms to electrical energy per unit charge
  2. B1 (p.d.): energy transferred from electrical energy to other forms per unit charge
  3. Both need "per unit charge"; the direction of the energy transfer is what distinguishes them.
Worked exampleWorked example

State two differences between stationary and progressive waves [2]

  1. B1 (Amplitude): in a stationary wave the amplitude varies between nodes; in a progressive wave all points have the same amplitude.
  2. B1 (Phase): between two adjacent nodes all points are in phase; in a progressive wave adjacent points are not in phase.
  3. Alternative B1 (Energy): a stationary wave does not transmit energy; a progressive wave does.
Write both sidesEach difference must say what happens in both waves to earn the mark.
